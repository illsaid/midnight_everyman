Not stairs. A chain. Steps bolted to it, running over a sprocket at each end and then — [thoughtful] carrying on. Underneath the floor. Upside down. Every step you can see, and as many again below you that you can't.

A conveyor belt, powerful enough to carry a crowd uphill. It simply happens to be shaped like a staircase.

And everywhere it can bite has something watching it.

[short pause] Follow one step to the landing. It is about to disappear into the floor, and the floor is not moving. That is the narrowest gap in the machine, and it is where a shoelace ends up.

Those ridges on the step are not grip. They are grooves — and the yellow plate has teeth that sit down inside them. Anything lying flat gets lifted up and over the teeth instead of going under with the step.

And if something does jam, the plate itself moves. Barely. A few millimetres.

[dryly] That is enough. The floor is a switch.

[short pause] Now the sides. Between the moving step and the fixed panel there is a gap. There has to be — one is moving, the other isn't. It is also about the width of the edge of a trainer.

So: brushes. That stiff strip at ankle height.

[flatly] They are not cleaning your shoes. They are there to make you move your foot. A brush on your ankle is a message, and the message is that you are too close to the edge. Ignore it, let something be dragged in anyway, and there is a switch behind that panel too.

[dryly] The brush is the warning. The switch is the apology.

[short pause] Every step is held flat by two tracks — one under the front wheels, one under the back. Climbing, they pull apart and the step tilts into a stair. At the top they converge and it flattens. The step never stops being level. It just stops being a stair.

Now break a wheel. The step sags. The problem is not the broken stair under your feet. It is that the broken stair is still being pulled towards the comb, where it will strike the teeth or leave a hole in the floor.

So the machine measures its own steps as they pass. Height. Position. Whether each one is still there. If one rides low, or has gone, it stops before that step reaches anybody.

[thoughtful] Back to the top, then. Going down. Full.

The motor is not driving that. It is holding it back. All that weight is going downhill, and the only reason it descends at a walking pace rather than a falling one is that a machine at the top refuses to let go.

Take that away. A chain breaks, a coupling lets go — and the load is still there. Fifty people, still going downhill, nothing holding them.

This is the one an escalator fears most, so it carries the most layers. A brake on the drive. On bigger machines, a second, independent brake gripping the main shaft. A governor that trips if the steps run twenty per cent fast. A sensor on the drive chain itself. And a red button at both ends, for a human who can see what is happening.

[short pause] Five things, with different triggers and different ways of failing. [flatly] That is not belt and braces. That is the design.

[long pause] So here is how it actually goes wrong.

Hong Kong, two thousand and seventeen. A long mall escalator, loaded. The main drive chain snaps — metal fatigue. That is fault one, and precisely what the drive chain sensor exists to catch. It should have dropped the auxiliary brake.

It didn't. Investigators found that mechanism gummed with old grease and dust, and one of its two springs clamped by a nut. [flatly] About half the force it needed to move.

The escalator ran backwards under the weight of the people standing on it.

[dryly] The chain broke. But the accident happened when the chain found out nobody was watching.
