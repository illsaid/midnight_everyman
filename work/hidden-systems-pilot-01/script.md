## Script

You've seen it in movies a hundred times. Someone holds a lighter up to a sprinkler head. The entire building erupts in a wild torrent of water — every sprinkler head, all at once. Everyone is soaked, papers fly, and the hero escapes in the confusion. It's a wonderful scene. It is also complete nonsense. Here is what actually happens. One sprinkler head opens. That one. The one directly above the fire. Every other head on that ceiling stays sealed, dry, and entirely uninterested. So the question isn't how they all know. It's what is inside that one, that isn't inside the other three hundred. Look up. There it is: a small brass fitting you have walked underneath ten thousand times without once considering it. Above it, a pipe. Inside the pipe, water — permanently at high pressure, sitting directly over your head, and it has been there since the day the building went up. Holding all of it back is a glass bulb about the size of a grain of rice. The head has four parts. A frame, screwed into the pipe. A cap, sealing the opening. A deflector — that flat, notched disc on the bottom. And wedged between the cap and the frame, under permanent compression, the bulb. Inside it: a glycerine-based liquid, and one small bubble of air. Heat the liquid and it expands. The bubble shrinks. Keep heating, and the bubble disappears altogether — at which point the liquid, having nowhere left to go, shatters the glass from the inside. The cap drops away. The water, which has been waiting patiently for about thirty years, leaves. It strikes the deflector, breaks into a cone of spray, and covers a patch of floor a few metres across. That is the whole mechanism. No sensor. No wiring. No computer. No control panel. No decision of any kind. Which is exactly why the film version cannot happen. Every head is its own separate machine. Each one holds its own bulb, and each bulb cares about precisely one thing: the temperature of the air immediately around it. A fire in the corner of an office is not a building-wide event. It is a local event. In a real fire, the usual number of heads that open is one. Occasionally two. They're even colour-coded, because of course they are. Orange opens at fifty-seven degrees. Red at sixty-eight. Yellow, seventy-nine. Green, ninety-three. Then blue, purple and black, for rooms where ninety-three degrees is considered a normal Tuesday. And that colour matters more than it looks — but the bulb has to break first. So. A bin ignites. Smoke rises, but the smoke does nothing at all here. Sprinklers cannot smell. They react to heat, and only heat. The hot gas spreads out across the ceiling and reaches the nearest head. The liquid begins to expand. The bubble shrinks. Ten seconds. Twenty. The glass is now under enormous internal pressure and it is holding — because glass, it turns out, is remarkably stubborn. Until it isn't. The bulb bursts. The cap falls. Water hits the deflector, comes down in a cone, and the fire, which had been roughly doubling in size every half minute, stops doubling. Two metres away, the next head sits in the cool air doing absolutely nothing. It will still be doing nothing when the fire brigade arrives. Now — that colour code. It exists because somebody has to choose the bulb for the room. Put an office-rated head above a deep fat fryer and it opens every lunchtime. Put a kitchen-rated head in a quiet office and the room can be well alight before it notices anything unusual. So the temperature isn't a setting. It's a judgement, made years ago, by someone who had to guess how hot your ceiling gets on an ordinary day. And when one does finally go, it doesn't reset. There's no switch. That head is finished. Somebody arrives with a spanner, screws in a new one, and the new one starts waiting too. Around forty million of these are fitted every year, and where they exist, they control the fire on their own the vast majority of the time. As for the water damage everybody worries about — a sprinkler head puts out somewhere around sixty litres a minute. A firefighter's hose puts out ten to twenty times that. The sprinkler isn't the flood. The sprinkler is what prevents the flood. So the ceiling above you is not one system. It's several hundred entirely separate ones, each with its own small glass fuse, each minding its own business, none of them talking to each other. Nothing up there is watching you. Nothing is deciding anything.

It is a piece of glass, holding back a river, waiting for something to get warm.

Look up. It's still there.

NFPA and the Home Fire Sprinkler Coalition before treating any of them as fact.
