3 · Critical difference 60 145 Comparative. The sentence the whole episode turns on.
4 · Run the event 95 230 Present tense, sequential. Thinnest words-per-second in the episode — the picture is doing the work.

6 · What matters 40 95 Second person again. One thing to remember, no summary.
Every sentence names something on screen If a sentence has no visual referent, either cut it or draw the thing. This one rule is what keeps a six-minute explainer from becoming a podcast with pictures.
Caption and narration never overlap The four or five caption lines say what the voice does not. Reading along with the narrator is the fastest way to feel talked down to.
Pose, then delay The question from beat 1 is not answered until beat 4 — but every movement in between must pay out something. Delay without morsels is the banana-bread failure: stringing the viewer along is what YouTube measures as dissatisfaction.
One re-hook per movement boundary Twelve boundaries, twelve single-sentence forward promises. Not a recap — a reason the next thirty seconds exist.
Statement, then intrigue Say what the episode covers, then add one sentence of unresolved specificity — a date, a name, a number. “A B-25 hit the Empire State Building in 1945” beats “something surprising happens”.
Seamless flow, no signposts No visible seam between the open and the body. Bridge with “starting with”, never announce with “let's get into it”. The whole script should read as one long sentence. Also: no chapters — we are narrative-shaped, and chapters invite a skip to beat 4.
No number without a comparison “1.15 metres per second” means nothing. “Walking pace” means something. The ledger holds the figure; the voice holds the comparison.
Read aloud, timed, then cut 15% Written prose always runs long. Draft → read against a stopwatch → cut → lock. Only then does anything get animated.
