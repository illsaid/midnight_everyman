# Retention structure

How an episode is built to be *stayed with*, as distinct from how it is built to
be understood. Comprehension is `production-workflow.md` Phase 2. This is the
other half, and until now it existed only as scattered rules on the diagram-layer
Script sheet and three techniques that were never written down at all.

**The governing constraint:** YouTube's native thumbnail test optimises
watch-time share, not click-through. A video that earns the click and loses the
viewer is worse than one that does neither, because it teaches the system to
stop showing you.

---

## The four layers

"Beat" is overloaded in this project. Fix the vocabulary first.

| Layer | Count | Length | Defined in |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Promise** | 1 | whole episode | `01-product/packaging.md` |
| **Narrative beat** | 6 | 25–95 s | diagram layer, sheet 07 |
| **Movement** | ~13 | 480–1080 frames (20–45 s) | diagram layer, sheet 06 |
| **Shot beat** | many | 3–5 s | `02-creative/visual-language.md` |

Retention devices live at **movement boundaries**. There are twelve of them in a
thirteen-movement episode, and each one is a place the viewer can leave.

---

## The skeleton comes before the script

Plan the devices on one page *before* writing prose. Galloway's phrasing:
*"manufacturing ahead of time is the ultimate thing."* Retention discovered
during drafting is retention you got lucky on.

The skeleton is a single table: movement number, what the viewer learns, and
which device carries them into the next movement. Nothing else. It should fit on
one screen and take fifteen minutes.

---

## Start at the end — the cold open

**The episode opens on the payoff moment, not on the setup.**

This was implicit in the documents and stated nowhere, and the two halves
contradicted each other:

- `01-product/packaging.md` says the first eight seconds are the thumbnail
  moment, animated — and the thumbnail moment is by rule *paused in action*, an
  instant before something resolves.
- The diagram-layer Script sheet says beat 1 "poses the question and does not
  answer it."

Both cannot be true. If the thumbnail is a bulb bursting and the first eight
seconds are that image in motion, the episode **opens on the moment the payoff
happens**, then rewinds. That is a cold open, and the beat table had no slot
for it.

### The slot

| | Runtime | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **Cold open** | first 8–15 s | Open the gap. Must *contain* the thumbnail image, need not open on it. |
| **Beat 1 remainder** | remainder of beat 1 | Rewind. Establish the ordinary situation the viewer is standing in. |

No change to the beat budget — the cold open is carved out of beat 1.

### Two ways to open, and how to choose

**A · Open on the false belief.** State the thing the viewer already believes,
in their words, as though agreeing with it — then break it. The thumbnail image
arrives a few seconds later as the correction.

**B · Open on the mechanism.** The thumbnail image is frame one, and the words
frame it afterwards.

**A beats B whenever the subject was chosen for a belief correction**, which for
this channel is most of the time. B assumes the viewer is already curious about
the object; A requires no curiosity at all, because it hooks on something they
have already seen and enjoyed. The sprinkler is the worked case: nobody arrives
curious about a brass fitting, but everyone has watched a heist film.

**The refinement this forces.** An earlier version of this rule said the first
frame *is* the thumbnail. Too rigid — it forbids opening on the belief. The
correct rule is:

> The thumbnail image must appear **within the first 8–15 seconds, as the
> resolution of the cold open.** It need not be frame one.

Click confirmation is preserved either way; the viewer still gets the image they
clicked for, and in form A they get it as a payoff rather than as a preamble.

**Test for form A:** can you state the false belief in one sentence a stranger
would nod along to? If they would not nod, there is no belief to break and you
are in form B.

### Why it works here specifically

The cold open shows the *what* and withholds the *why*. The viewer has seen the
bulb burst; they do not know that it is the only one that will. Beat 1 can then
rewind without losing tension, because the question has changed from "what
happens" to "why only that one."

A cold open is not a spoiler. Withholding the image the thumbnail already
promised is the actual failure — the viewer clicked for it and the first thing
they get is a preamble.

## Write the episode backwards

A structural point and a process point, and the process one matters more here.

The thumbnail moment is selected at **gate 3, before the script exists**. The
payoff image is therefore already fixed when writing begins. So write backwards
from it:

1. The payoff image is given. It is the thumbnail.
2. Ask what the viewer must already understand for that image to land.
3. Ask what they must understand for *that*.
4. Continue until you reach something they already know.
5. That is movement 1. Now write forwards.

Anything the chain does not require is cut, however interesting. This is what
stops a six-minute explainer becoming a list of facts about an object — the test
for every sentence is not "is this true" but "does the payoff need it."

## The device inventory

**0 · Cold open** — first 8 seconds.
The thumbnail moment, animated, before any framing. Shown, not said.

**1 · Statement, then intrigue** — movement 1, after the cold open.
Say what the episode covers, then one sentence of unresolved specificity. A date,
a name, a number. *"A B-25 hit the Empire State Building in 1945"* beats
*"something surprising happens."* Specificity is the defence against generic
explainer.

**2 · Re-hook** — every movement boundary.
One sentence, forward-facing. Not a recap. A reason the next thirty seconds
exist. Twelve boundaries, twelve re-hooks.

**3 · Soon X, first Y** — two or three times per episode.
Name a question you will return to, then deliberately go elsewhere.
**Both halves must be interesting.** Done performatively this is the
"stay tuned for number five" cliché and it is worse than nothing. Done quietly —
*"That colour coding matters more than it looks, but first the bulb has to
break"* — it just works.

**4 · The morsel** — every movement, without exception.
The central question is not answered until beat 4. Every movement before it must
still pay out something small and complete. Delay without morsels is the
**banana-bread failure**: the recipe blog that tells you the history of banana
bread while you stand in front of the oven. Stringing the viewer along is what
YouTube measures as dissatisfaction.

**5 · The hold** — once, immediately before the payoff.
Ten frames of complete stillness before the failure fires. Nothing animating,
nothing marching. The cheapest tension the format has.

**6 · Seamless flow** — continuous.
No visible seam between the open and the body. Bridge with "starting with";
never announce with "let's get into it." The whole script should read as one long
sentence.

**7 · The thirty-second rule** — continuous.
No frame state holds longer than 720 frames without a camera move, a state
change, or a new element drawing in. Stillness is where retention dies.

## When there is no natural escalation, completion is the payoff

Some formats build — a race gets closer, a build gets finished. An explainer does
not naturally escalate; a mechanism is not more exciting at minute four than at
minute two just because time has passed.

The available substitute is **completion**. The promise is that by the end the
viewer will hold the whole thing: every part named, assembled, and run once from
beginning to end. That is why beat 4 runs the event in sequence rather than
summarising it, and why beat 6 states one thing to remember rather than
recapping six.

Two consequences:

- **Never leave a named part unused.** If it entered the ledger it must do
  something before the end, or it should not have been named.
- **The last movement must feel like a click, not a stop.** The viewer should be
  able to say what the machine does, in one sentence, without help.

## The invisible focus group test

The acceptance test for a skeleton. Pause at roughly 1, 2 and 4 minutes and ask:
*if I put the audience in a room right now, what would they say is coming?*

| Answer | Verdict |
|---|---|
| "He mentioned a thing I can't get out of my head" | Pass |
| "He's shown me the parts, now he has to show me it working" | Pass |
| "I don't really know. It's just kind of flowing" | **Fail — add a device at the preceding boundary** |

Run this on the skeleton, not on the finished edit. It is free before the script
and expensive after the render.

---

## Chapters

**Do not use them.** Chapters suit tutorials, listicles and podcasts, where the
viewer is entitled to skip to the part they need. This format is narrative-shaped
and has a payoff at beat 4; chapters invite a skip straight to it and destroy
the delay the whole structure is built on.

---

## Anti-patterns

| Never | Why |
|---|---|
| "Let's get into it" / "but first" / "in this video" | Signposts a seam that should not exist |
| "Keep an eye out for number five" | Performative foreshadowing; reads as manipulation |
| A recap at a movement boundary | Backward-facing where the viewer needs forward |
| Withholding the mechanism to reach a runtime | The banana-bread failure |
| Any ask before beat 6 | Spends goodwill before it is earned |

## Worked skeleton — fire sprinkler pilot

The drafted script runs ~665 words / 4 m 35 s across ten movements.

| # | Viewer learns | Device carrying into next |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | **Cold open, form A** — the heist-film scene, then "complete nonsense", then the truth: one head open, the rest dry | The question: what is in *that* one that isn't in the other three hundred |
| 1 | Pressurised water is overhead, held by a glass bulb | Statement + intrigue — "since the day the building went up" |

| 3 | Four parts: frame, cap, deflector, bulb | Morsel — the bulb is under permanent compression |
| 4 | Liquid, bubble, expansion, shatter from the inside | Morsel — glass is stubborn until it isn't |
| 5 | Cap drops, water leaves, spray. No sensor, no decision | Re-hook — "which brings us to the part films get wrong" |
| 6 | Each head is its own machine. Local, not global | Morsel — the usual number is one |
| 7 | Colour code, 57 °C to 227 °C | Soon X, first Y — the code matters later |
| 8 | A bin ignites. Smoke does nothing — heat only | Re-hook — the gas layer is moving |
| 9 | Bubble shrinks, glass holds | **The hold** |
| 10 | Burst, cone, fire stops doubling. The neighbour does nothing | Payoff → consequences → close |

**Draft v2, 2 Sep 2026** — both deviations in v1 are resolved. A cold open was
added and the script extended to ~745 words / 5 m 08 s, clearing the 300-second
floor in `01-product/format.md`. The extension went into the colour-code
movement, which previously passed through without paying out: the code exists
because a head is chosen per room, and a kitchen-rated bulb in an office would
never open while an office-rated bulb over a fryer would open every lunchtime.

The central question is still resolved earlier than beat 4. With a cold open
that is now correct rather than a deviation: the cold open *is* the payoff image,
so the episode's tension moves from "what happens" to "why only that one," and
that question does hold to the end.
